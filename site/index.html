The model

The box: clear acrylic, 12 × 12 in, so you can see how it is built. The 7 in touchscreen sits in the sloped front, one panel from the table to the ceiling, facing up at you; the ceiling (6¾ in up inside, 11 Nov 2026) slides out the back. It runs on a 12 V rechargeable battery (TalentCell) along the left wall; its charging socket and the on/off switch are in the left wall. The whole back is a lift-off door held by magnets. Inside: the Raspberry Pi on the back of the screen, two converters on the right wall (7.5 V for the servos, 5 V for the Pi), and the servo driver board on the floor, front left, with one cable chain to all eight servos.

Grey = the gears: a 40-tooth gear on each servo and an identical one on its tube, 16 in all. Silver = each arm’s aluminium collar on its tube. Each arm is a straight 3/32 in rod out to a clear aquarium elbow, then an upright into the planet; heights and lengths are the measured ones (8 Nov 2026: Neptune’s tube top 8¼ in, its collar low, its arm ½ in under Uranus’s; 11 Nov 2026: the ceiling is now 6¾ in up, so set its real height and tell Claude). Wiring diagram

How the battery, switch, both converters, the Pi, the screen and the servos connect. Codes are the parts-list items.

Before powering on:

  1. Find the + wire of the cut splitter leg with a multimeter (don't trust the colours). The leg is a 2-wire cord: split and strip each wire about ⅜ in. Join + to a switch lead with a WAGO (or solder + heat-shrink).
  2. Set up the DROK (B10) as below, before the servos are connected.
  3. Driver board (A1) jumper on B (USB ↔ servo).
  4. The TalentCell's own switch stays ON; the box's switch (B7) turns everything on and off.

DROK (B10) setup, nothing on its output:

  1. Wire only its input (IN+ / IN−) to the 12 V rail and switch on. The screen shows IN volts (≈ 12 V).
  2. Short-press IN/OUT to show OUT volts. If it says OFF, short-press ON/OFF.
  3. Hold ON/OFF until it shows ON: the output then comes on by itself at every power-up. Skip this and the switch starts the Pi but not the servos.
  4. Turn the CV pot (left blue trimmer; CC on the right is the current limit) counter-clockwise to 7.5 V. It is multi-turn: many turns before the reading moves. Check with a multimeter on OUT+ / OUT−.
  5. Optional current limit: CC fully counter-clockwise (10+ turns), meter on its 10 A range straight across OUT+ / OUT−, turn CC clockwise to about 3 A. Then a jam dips only the servos instead of tripping the battery (and the Pi).
  6. Switch off, then OUT+ → driver board DC+, OUT− → DC− (the output has no reverse protection). OUT− goes only to the driver board, never also to the WAGO −.
  7. Short-press IN/OUT back to IN: the screen is then a rough battery gauge (12.6 V full, ≈ 9.5 V charge it).

Then check the Pi with vcgencmd get_throttled (should be 0x0) while all eight servos start, and again with the battery low.

Tested on the battery 3–4 Oct 2026: the switch cuts all power and switching back on brings everything up. DROK 7.5 V; about 1 A to the servos at full speed (1,000 years/min, no arms yet); Pi 0x0, 52 °C. If the screen stays on the desktop, the servo board's USB isn't seen: reseat the USB-C at the driver board.

What you are looking at

  1. On top (Stage 1): one slow motor in the box turns the wooden main rod. Four cross-rods pass through it, a planet on each end, Sun on top. Everything turns together. Simple, and on purpose not accurate.
  2. On the front (Stage 2): the date dial. Set a date with the buttons on the left and the screen shows it; on the disc of 8 LED rings each planet's light sits where that planet really is on that date. Sun = centre light.
  3. The small dial is the motor speed. The two halves share the box and the power strip, nothing else, so the top can be built first and the dial added later.
  4. Try it: press +1 mo a few times. Mercury's light (inner ring) jumps a third of a lap each time; Neptune's (outer ring) hardly moves.

How it works

  1. A fixed 1/8 in steel rod (the Sun post) stands in the middle of the base and holds the Sun.
  2. Around it sit 8 brass tubes, one inside the next, like a telescope; each turns on its own. A collar on the post carries the whole stack; each arm’s collar holds the tube below it down.
  3. At the top, each tube carries one planet’s arm. Outer tube = lowest arm = outermost planet, so the risers never meet an arm and all the planets share one level.
  4. Inside the box, each tube carries a 40-tooth gear, stacked like a staircase. Beside each one a servo on an adjustable stand drives it through an identical gear, so the servo turns exactly as far as its planet.
  5. The servos know their own position. All eight and their wiring stay in the box; nothing electrical goes up the column.

How one level drives its planet

  1. The centre tube is fixed. It never turns. The bus cable runs up inside it.
  2. A clamp grips the tube and holds a small plate; the servo is screwed to that plate. So the servo does not move either.
  3. The hub gear has two ball bearings in its centre hole and the tube passes through them: the gear spins freely around the fixed tube.
  4. The small servo gear meshes with the hub gear. Servo turns → hub gear turns.
  5. The arm is bolted flat onto the hub gear, and a thin riser rod stands up from the end of the arm, carrying the planet up to the shared orbital plane.
  6. Long arms sit low, short arms sit high, so a riser never meets another arm.
