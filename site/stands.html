Column and servo stands

The eight gears are glued on (7 Nov 2026), their levels ½ in apart. This page goes from there to every gear turning under its own servo: the Sun post into the base, the column stacked, each gear's wobble measured, the stands built, then one servo meshed and tested before the other seven. Will the wobble be covered by the teeth?

Mostly yes, if it's small. Here's how much room the teeth give you:

  • Each tooth is 2.25 mm tall. Meshed at 40.0 mm (1.575 in) between centres, the two gears' teeth overlap by 2 mm.
  • An off-centre gear sticks out further on one side. You set the servo in so that side (the tight spot) just clears. Half a turn later the teeth have pulled apart by the whole wobble.
  • The gears keep turning smoothly, with a tooth pair always pushing, until they've pulled apart by about ¾ mm (0.03 in). Past that, there are moments when only the tooth tips touch: clicks, jumps and worn teeth once the arms add load.

Your budget is the swing: the difference between a gear's closest and farthest point over one full turn. Printer paper is about 0.1 mm (0.004 in) thick, so you can measure it by counting strips of paper:

The budget covers everything together: the tube gear's wobble, the servo gear's wobble, and any sideways play in the tube. That's why the steps below measure each gear on the real column, take up Mercury's play on the rod, and set each servo at its gear's tight spot. These are textbook figures for module-1 teeth. Technic teeth have a little extra backlash, so the powered test in phase 5 is the real judge.

Trick: two wobbly gears can cancel. The pair is 1:1, so the same teeth meet on every turn and the two wobbles stay in step. Mark each gear's high spot with a dot (phases 3 and 5). Mesh them so the dots are mirror images across the line joining the two shafts. For example, the tube gear's dot points straight at the servo while the servo gear's dot points straight away from the column. Then the wobbles subtract instead of adding.
1
Sun post into the base¼ in base sheet, 1/8 in bit, 5-minute epoxy
  • Leave the protective film on. Everything below is measured from this edge.
  • Measure from both sides; the two marks should land on the same spot. (The printed template in phase 4 has the same point at its centre, and its top edge is the back edge, so you can use it here instead.)
  • Wrap tape round the bit 3/16 in from its tip as a depth flag. Scrap wood underneath, slow speed, light pressure, drill held upright. The sheet is only ¼ in thick, so stop at the flag.
  • A little epoxy in the hole, push the rod to the bottom, wipe off what squeezes out. Check it's square from two directions at right angles to each other, and prop it there (two blocks or books) while it sets. Handle after about an hour; leave it overnight before you stack the column on it.
  • This carries the whole column. You can slide it later to raise or lower everything.
2
Stack the column, and hold its topWashers, light oil, the ¼ in top sheet
Mercury's play matters here. The rod is about 1/32 in (0.9 mm) narrower than the inside of Mercury's tube. On its own, that is more than the whole wobble budget, and every other tube rides on Mercury. Take it up before you measure anything.
  • Lift Mercury off. Wrap the rod just above the collar (inside Mercury's gear end) and again near Mercury's top, one wrap at a time, until the tube slides on with no side play but still turns freely. Thread-seal (PTFE plumber's) tape is best, because it's slippery. Masking tape works too. Then wiggle Mercury's gear sideways: you should barely feel it move.
  • Washers (none under Mercury): Venus M5 · Earth M5 drilled 7/32 · Mars M6 · Jupiter M6 drilled 9/32 · Saturn M6 drilled 5/16 · Uranus M8 drilled 11/32 · Neptune M8 drilled 3/8. A drop of light oil between tubes is fine now that the gears are glued; wipe any off the gear faces.
  • In the finished box, the lid's hole holds the column's top. Without it, the 16 in rod flexes, and a servo pushing on an upper gear moves it sideways by more than the whole budget. You need this hole anyway:
    • Cut the top (11.75 × 9⅛) if it isn't cut yet. Mark the hole 6 in from either side and 3¾ in from the back edge. Open it with the step bit to 3/8, then the 13/32 twist bit, slowly, scrap wood under. Ream until Neptune's tube turns freely with no rattle.
    • Slide the lid down over Neptune's tube and rest its corners on four temporary legs (blocks, cans or books) so its underside is about 7⅜ in above the base. Keep the legs at the corners, out of the servos' way.
    • Check the column stands upright with a square from two sides, then tape the legs down.
3
Measure each gear's wobbleOn the real column: a flat block, paper strips, a fine marker

You need a heavy block with a flat, square side (a wood block, a steel square on its edge, a stack of books), printer paper cut into strips about ½ in wide, and a fine marker.

  • Stand the block on the base with its flat side just touching the gear's tooth tips. Turn the tube slowly, one full turn. If a tooth catches, ease the block out until the gear only just brushes past at its tightest point. Push lightly: a hard push shifts the tube on its play and spoils the reading.
  • Put the dot on the gear's top face, out at the teeth, not near the hole (the washer sits there). That dot is the gear's high spot.
  • Slide strips one at a time between the tooth tips and the block until they're snug. Type the count in the table. Also watch the gear's face while it turns: it should stay level to within about 1 mm. A gear glued on at a tilt wobbles up and down too.
  • Any order. Go round the column with the block; the lower gears are easier to reach before the servos go in.

Worst case adds the two swings; Dots matched uses the trick at the top (the difference between them). Mercury's row also needs its rod play taken up (phase 2).

A gear marked Fix? Try the free fixes first: re-centre its servo gear (phase 5), match the dots, and set the mesh at the tight spot. If it's still past 0.65 mm, swap that tube's gear: you have 8 spare 40-tooth gears. Lift the tubes outside it off the column. Warm the joint with a hair dryer for a minute or two, then twist the gear off with pliers on the gear, never on the tube. If it won't come, cut the gear off with flush cutters without squeezing the tube. Scrape the old epoxy off the brass. Drill the new gear one size small and ream it to a snug slide that doesn't rock. Glue it at the same mark, and while the epoxy is still soft, turn it against the block and nudge it centred.

5
One servo first: MercuryThe worst case: if it passes, the others will
The servos can't be turned by hand. Their gearbox is 345:1, so once a servo gear is meshed, the tube won't turn by hand either: don't force it. Claude turns them for you, one at a time, from the Pi. Ask in plain words, for example spin Mercury's servo slowly. (For Claude: stop the orrery service, then pi/servo_tool.py spin 1 --rpm 3; it prints the load. goto 1 <deg> points it.)
  • Before mounting, with the servo on the bench, ask Claude to spin it slowly. Hold the block against its teeth as in phase 3. The four M3 screws allow about ±0.9 mm of play, so if the swing is more than 2–3 sheets, loosen them, nudge the gear toward the side that's furthest out, tighten, and check again. Dot the high spot and type the swing into the table in phase 3.
  • Thumb nut, then a ¼ in SAE washer, under the plate; a plain nut on top (no washer: there's no room for one beside the servo). Ease the two rods apart a little to drop the plate on. Turn each top nut a full turn to check its corners clear the servo.
  • Lay a steel ruler across both top faces. Turn both thumb nuts the same amount (0.8 mm a turn) until the ruler sits flat on both.
  • Turn the tube (it's still free) so its dot points straight at the servo. Ask Claude to point the servo gear's dot straight away from the column. Mirror images, as in the trick at the top.
  • Hold a strip of printer paper between the teeth where they meet. It should be just gripped. Too loose or too tight: take the plate off, ease both rod holes a little toward (or away from) the column, and try again. Then tighten both top nuts down on the plate. Ask Claude to turn the servo slowly, and the paper rolls out through the teeth. That leaves about a sheet of paper's clearance at the tight spot.
  • Watch and listen. You want the tube turning steadily with no click, jump or pause, no groan at one spot in every turn, and the tube not shifting sideways. Claude watches the servo's load: one bump per turn means a tight spot. If it's tight somewhere, loosen the lock nuts, slide out a hair, and redo the paper with that spot facing the servo.
  • While it spins, pinch the tube lightly between finger and thumb. It should keep turning without clicking. The servo is very strong, so a light pinch is all you need.
  • If it's clockwise, the software's ARM_DIR needs flipping. Tell Claude.
  • Then you can see if it ever creeps.

The other seven, then all togetherSame steps, working up from Venus
  • Centre and dot the servo gear, mount it, level the faces, line up the dots, set the mesh with paper, run it slow and fast, then lock it.
  • Its gear screws are closest to the lid: check they clear it (about ¼ in) as it turns.
  • HEALTH shows all eight connected. Every tube turns smoothly, and none drags its neighbour. There are no arms yet, so this is the cleanest test the gears will ever get: any click now will be worse with the planets on.
